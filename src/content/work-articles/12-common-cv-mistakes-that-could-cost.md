<p> </p><h1>Common CV Mistakes That Could Cost You an Interview</h1><p></p><div><a href="https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEiYSDn1Ua0kPSMsjjbNCMMqy83EAye2ezNaIvjfWq4iuNe8D4_zaZyakyvn6n6nFByrDEVo7Pyfmc0u3xlrygutNbNOXtxAND3znGU-RDpymrPRQNbqyO0LGD4tAKPZUMuE1wd5rw7tzior0eA1vrTgk98aU4rVnOLVaQSb085fLlJXO36_8EFeWJWVVMs/s1600/Gemini_Generated_Image_dh8rd6dh8rd6dh8r%20%282%29%20%281%29.jpeg"></a></div><br><br><p></p><p>A job seeker can meet most of the requirements in a vacancy and still struggle to get an interview. The problem may not be a lack of qualifications or experience. It may be that the CV fails to make either one obvious.</p><p>Recruiters rarely have the luxury of spending several minutes deciphering every application. When vacancies attract dozens or hundreds of applicants, a document that is poorly organised, vague about experience or missing essential information can be overlooked before the candidate has a chance to explain themselves.</p><p>For job seekers in Uganda, where applicants may be competing for graduate trainee positions, administrative jobs, customer service roles and opportunities with established companies, that first impression matters. Yet many CV problems are surprisingly ordinary. They have less to do with sophisticated design or impressive vocabulary than with the information an employer needs to make a decision.</p><h2>A CV that tries to fit every job often fits none particularly well</h2><p>Keeping a general CV is useful. Sending the same version to every employer is a different matter.</p><p>A sales employer may want evidence of customer relationships, negotiation and meeting targets. An administrative role may require accurate record-keeping, scheduling and correspondence. An entry-level IT position may place greater value on troubleshooting skills, technical projects and familiarity with particular software.</p><p>An applicant could possess all these abilities and still submit a CV that makes none of them stand out.</p><p>The answer is not to rewrite your employment history for every vacancy. It is to change the emphasis. Read the job description, identify its most important requirements and bring your relevant experience towards the top of the document. If an employer asks for Excel proficiency, for example, explain what you can do with Excel rather than relying on a vague reference to computer literacy.</p><p>The opening paragraph deserves particular attention. Statements such as “hard-working, ambitious and highly motivated” occupy valuable space without telling an employer much.</p><p>Compare that with a profile describing a customer service applicant who has two years of experience handling enquiries, resolving routine complaints and maintaining sales records. The latter gives a recruiter something specific to assess.</p><p>A recent graduate can take the same approach without inventing professional experience. Relevant coursework, internships, university projects and volunteer work can demonstrate useful abilities when formal employment is limited.</p><h2>The difference between having experience and proving it</h2><p>One of the most persistent weaknesses in CVs is the long list of responsibilities that reveals very little about the applicant's performance.</p><p>“Responsible for customer service” might describe someone who answered occasional enquiries or someone who handled difficult complaints throughout a busy working day. The phrase leaves the employer to guess.</p><p>A stronger description explains the work: handled customer enquiries, recorded complaints and referred unresolved cases to the appropriate department. Where reliable figures are available, the applicant might also mention transaction volumes, sales targets or improvements they helped achieve.</p><p>The distinction matters because employers are not simply looking for people who have occupied certain positions. They want evidence that candidates can handle the work being offered.</p><p>This is also where applicants sometimes go too far. Inflated percentages, invented achievements and exaggerated job titles may make a CV sound impressive, but they create a problem when an interviewer asks for details. An ordinary achievement explained clearly is more credible than a remarkable one that cannot be defended.</p><p>For someone with limited employment history, evidence need not come from a paid job. A student who built a functioning website, managed records for a community organisation or coordinated a university project has something concrete to discuss. The value lies in explaining what they actually did, which tools they used and what the work involved.</p><p>A CV should make genuine experience visible, not manufacture experience that does not exist.</p><h2>Small inconsistencies can undermine an otherwise strong application</h2><p>Employment dates, qualification titles and job descriptions may seem like administrative details, but they help an employer establish whether the account is credible.</p><p>A CV that says a candidate worked in two full-time positions simultaneously, gives conflicting graduation dates or claims a qualification that is still in progress creates unnecessary uncertainty. Sometimes there is a perfectly reasonable explanation. The problem is that an unclear document forces the recruiter to investigate before they can properly assess the applicant.</p><p>Use a consistent month-and-year format for employment and education. List recent experience first, and describe qualifications accurately. If a degree is unfinished, say so and provide an expected completion date where appropriate.</p><p>Employment gaps are not automatically disqualifying. People spend time studying, searching for work, caring for relatives or managing other circumstances. A brief, factual explanation may help where the gap is significant, but there is no need to disclose private medical or family details simply to justify an interruption in employment.</p><p>The same principle applies to skills and professional credentials. Do not claim advanced Excel, fluency in a language, a professional licence or a completed degree unless the claim is accurate. Some employers verify academic records, contact previous employers or test technical abilities during recruitment. A CV that creates expectations the applicant cannot meet can do more harm than one that presents modest experience honestly.</p><h2>The temptation to make a CV look impressive</h2><p>Design has its place. A well-presented CV can make information easier to navigate, particularly in creative professions where visual communication is part of the work. But elaborate formatting is not a substitute for relevant content.</p><p>Multiple columns, decorative icons, text boxes and graphics can make a document harder to read on a phone or interfere with the way some applicant tracking systems extract information. These systems are used by certain employers to organise applications; they are not universal, and no particular template guarantees that a CV will pass every screening process.</p><p>For most applicants, a simple layout is the sensible starting point. Use clear headings, readable fonts, consistent spacing and straightforward bullet points. Keep contact details visible and ensure that essential information is not buried inside images or complicated design elements.</p><p>PDF is generally a useful format because it preserves the document's layout, but the employer's instructions take priority. If a vacancy requests a Word document or an online form, follow that requirement.</p><p>Length also needs judgement. A recent graduate will often have enough relevant material for one or two pages. An experienced professional may need more space to explain substantial responsibilities, projects and qualifications. Neither a rigid one-page rule nor a lengthy document full of unrelated details serves every applicant equally well.</p><p>The real test is whether each section earns its place. An old software package, a string of unrelated short courses or several paragraphs describing routine duties may add pages without helping the employer understand your suitability.</p><h2>Personal information belongs in the right place</h2><p>Applicants sometimes include far more personal information than a general CV requires. A National Identification Number, passport number, bank details or full residential address rarely helps a recruiter assess professional competence.</p><p>For most ordinary applications, a name, working telephone number, professional email address and relevant location are sufficient. Stating “Kampala, Uganda”, for instance, may be enough when an employer needs to know where a candidate is based.</p><p>A photograph, marital status or date of birth should not be included automatically unless the employer has a legitimate, stated reason for requesting the information. Requirements can differ between roles and recruitment processes.</p><p>Supporting documents are a separate matter. An employer may legitimately request academic transcripts, identification or professional certificates at an appropriate stage. Before sharing them, verify the organisation and make sure the submission channel is genuine. A request for a CV is not a reason to send passwords, mobile-money PINs or one-time verification codes.</p><p>This caution is particularly important when responding to vacancies circulated through social media or messaging groups. Check the original advertisement and use a verified employer contact or recruitment portal whenever possible.</p><h2>Sometimes the CV is not the problem</h2><p>A strong CV can still fail to produce an interview. An applicant may lack a required licence, fall short of the stated experience threshold or be competing against candidates whose backgrounds are a closer match. Recruitment decisions also depend on circumstances the applicant cannot see.</p><p>Nevertheless, some applications are weakened by mistakes that have nothing to do with qualifications. A wrong telephone number can make an applicant unreachable. A missing attachment can leave an application incomplete. An email sent without the required vacancy reference may be harder to process, while a document containing another employer's name suggests that the applicant has rushed the submission.</p><p>These errors are avoidable. Before applying, check the vacancy's requirements, requested file format, supporting documents, destination address and deadline. Open the final CV after exporting it, inspect every page and test any links. Read the application email before sending it, and confirm that the promised attachments are actually included.</p><p>A second reader can be useful, particularly after several rounds of editing. Ask them to identify the job you are targeting, your strongest relevant skills and the evidence supporting your suitability. If they struggle to answer, the document may be too vague or poorly organised.</p><p>For applicants who have submitted several applications without receiving interview invitations, reviewing recent vacancies can reveal a pattern. Perhaps the CV does not demonstrate a frequently requested skill, or perhaps the candidate is applying for positions that require experience they have not yet acquired. Revising the document helps only when the underlying problem is understood.</p><p>A CV cannot guarantee an interview, and presentation cannot compensate for a qualification a position genuinely requires. But a clear, accurate and relevant document gives an employer fewer reasons to overlook an applicant before considering what they can offer.</p><p>That is the point of a good CV: not to make a candidate look perfect, but to make their real suitability difficult to miss.</p><div aria-label="Article tags"><span>CV</span><span>CV writing</span><span>career advice</span><span>job search</span><span>Employment in Uganda</span><span>Job Applications</span></div><div>Content originally published on SSEGU.
