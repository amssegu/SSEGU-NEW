<h2> How to Tailor Your CV for Different Jobs Without Starting from Scratch</h2><p></p><div><a href="https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEgU0R-M4f4cqOM1qvWv64t6WlQu2wKz-dnSRP8dpq1XHWssfOG5j-GEn_Bu4SH3PqJADjqjDYFNFTFx1fSMylmJZe1tDj8_DNOT2-IV3ECROmXiYORwMf_vRJsySJzTIenHxgpBvbfmX121V_EqQ643Y3Y2sTVGhlJ2F7KKmnU_XVWK6EXu38RCiNHCY_E/s1600/Gemini_Generated_Image_dh8rd6dh8rd6dh8r%20%281%29.jpeg"></a></div><br><p></p><p>A CV can be perfectly accurate and still be a poor fit for the job in front of it.</p><p>Imagine two people applying for different positions with similar work histories. Both have worked in retail, handled customer enquiries, maintained records and helped with stock management. One is applying for a customer service role; the other wants an administrative job. If they submit identical CVs, neither necessarily presents the strongest case for being hired.</p><p>The customer service employer wants evidence of communication, patience and problem-solving. The administrative recruiter is more interested in accurate records, organisation and familiarity with office tools. The candidates may possess all these abilities, but a CV that gives them equal prominence leaves the employer to work out which ones matter.</p><p>This is why tailoring your CV matters. It is not about inventing a new professional identity for every vacancy. It is about making the most relevant parts of your experience easier to recognise.</p><p>For jobseekers applying to several employers, including those navigating Uganda's competitive job market, that distinction can save time and produce stronger applications.</p><h2>The job description is more useful than your old CV</h2><p>Many applicants begin by opening their existing CV and making a few cosmetic changes. They replace the job title in the profile, add a sentence about being hardworking and send the document off.</p><p>A better starting point is the vacancy itself.</p><p>Read the responsibilities and requirements as a description of the employer's immediate needs. A company recruiting an accounts assistant may need someone who can reconcile records, work with spreadsheets and handle financial information carefully. A hotel recruiting a front-desk receptionist may prioritise guest communication, reservations and the ability to remain composed when several people need attention at once.</p><p>The difference is not merely the job title. It is the evidence each employer needs before feeling confident about a candidate.</p><p>Look for requirements that appear repeatedly or are central to the work. Separate those from desirable extras, and distinguish both from compulsory qualifications or licences. If an employer requires a particular professional credential, no amount of clever wording can legitimately replace it.</p><p>It helps to make a small comparison before editing:</p><table><tbody><tr><th>What the vacancy asks for</th><th>Evidence from your background</th></tr><tr><td>Customer communication</td><td>Responded to enquiries and followed up on complaints</td></tr><tr><td>Spreadsheet skills</td><td>Maintained weekly stock records in Excel</td></tr><tr><td>Accurate documentation</td><td>Checked entries and updated business records</td></tr><tr><td>Organisation</td><td>Coordinated tasks and prepared routine reports</td></tr></tbody></table><p>These examples are illustrative. The point is to connect each requirement to something you have genuinely done. If you cannot identify supporting evidence, consider whether the skill is essential, whether you have a related transferable skill, or whether the gap makes you unsuitable for the role.</p><p>That exercise is more valuable than copying every keyword from the advertisement into your CV.</p><h2>The same experience can tell different stories</h2><p>Consider a retail worker who serves customers, records sales, checks stock and prepares daily summaries. Those responsibilities may seem ordinary, particularly to someone who has performed them for years. Yet they can support applications in several fields.</p><p>For a customer service position, the worker could emphasise answering questions, explaining products and helping resolve complaints. For an administrative role, maintaining records and preparing summaries become more important. For a stock control position, checking inventory and identifying discrepancies deserve greater attention.</p><p>The underlying employment history stays the same. What changes is the selection and order of evidence.</p><p>This is also where many applicants make an avoidable mistake: they describe duties without showing what those duties involved.</p><p>“Responsible for stock records” tells a recruiter very little. “Maintained weekly stock records in Excel, checked entries for missing information and prepared summaries for the supervisor” gives a clearer picture of the work.</p><p>A measurable result can strengthen the description when one exists. If you genuinely managed records for 200 product lines, for example, that detail communicates the scope of your responsibility. But numbers should be used because they are meaningful and verifiable, not because a CV looks more impressive with percentages attached to every achievement.</p><p>You do not need to claim that you increased efficiency by 40 per cent if you never measured it. The ability to describe your work precisely is an advantage in its own right.</p><p>Nor should tailoring become a form of misrepresentation. Volunteer experience remains volunteer experience; an academic project does not become professional employment because the vacancy asks for experience. Employers may explore these claims during interviews, reference checks or practical assessments.</p><h2>Your opening paragraph has a job to do</h2><p>The profile at the top of a CV is often wasted on statements such as “hardworking, self-motivated individual seeking an opportunity in a reputable organisation”. The sentiment is harmless, but it could describe almost anyone applying for almost any job.</p><p>A stronger profile gives the reader a reason to continue.</p><p>For a candidate with relevant customer service experience, that might mean describing their experience handling enquiries, maintaining records and following up on unresolved issues. Someone applying for a first job may instead draw attention to relevant coursework, practical projects, volunteering or demonstrable digital skills.</p><p>The important thing is to be specific without becoming verbose.</p><p>The same principle applies to the skills section. An administrative applicant should not bury Excel, data entry and document management beneath a long list of unrelated technical interests. A web development applicant, on the other hand, should put relevant programming languages, frameworks and projects near the top.</p><p>This does not mean deleting everything that fails to match the vacancy. It means recognising that space and attention are limited. The first half of a CV should make the strongest relevant case for your application, rather than presenting your entire working life as though every detail carries equal weight.</p><h2>Keywords matter, but they cannot do the work for you</h2><p>Online applications have made the recruitment process more complicated. Some employers use applicant tracking systems (ATS) to collect applications, organise candidate information or search for particular qualifications and skills. Others rely more heavily on manual screening.</p><p>Consequently, terminology matters. If a vacancy asks for data entry and your experience genuinely includes data entry, use the term rather than hiding it behind a vague phrase such as “information handling”. If you have worked with a particular accounting package or customer relationship management system, name it where relevant.</p><p>But there is no universal collection of keywords that guarantees an interview. An ATS is not necessarily a machine that rejects every CV lacking an exact phrase, and different systems work differently.</p><p>A readable document with conventional headings such as “Work Experience”, “Education” and “Skills” is a sensible starting point. Elaborate layouts, graphics and multiple columns can make information harder for some systems to interpret. Follow the employer's instructions about file format, and keep contact details in the main body of the document.</p><p>Most importantly, keywords should describe actual competence. Listing advanced Excel skills when you can only enter information into a spreadsheet might help a CV resemble the advertisement, but it creates a problem the moment an interviewer asks you to demonstrate those skills.</p><h2>A career change requires a different kind of emphasis</h2><p>Tailoring becomes particularly important when your previous job title does not resemble the one you want next.</p><p>A factory worker applying for a warehouse position, for example, may have relevant experience with stock movement, procedures, safety requirements and accurate documentation. A person moving from retail into office administration may be able to demonstrate customer communication, record-keeping and the organisation of daily tasks.</p><p>These are transferable skills, but the connection must be made explicit. A recruiter should not have to guess how experience in one environment might apply in another.</p><p>For students and recent graduates, relevant projects can serve a similar purpose. A website built as part of a university assignment may demonstrate practical development skills. A spreadsheet project may show an ability to organise data. A volunteering role may provide evidence of coordination or communication.</p><p>Such experience should be labelled honestly and described in terms of what you actually accomplished. If a vacancy requires several years of professional experience, a personal project does not erase that requirement. It can, however, strengthen an application where the employer is open to candidates with equivalent practical ability or limited formal experience.</p><p>Qualifications deserve the same care. State the name of the institution and the qualification accurately, and distinguish an ongoing course from one you have completed. Where a Ugandan employer requires a recognised professional qualification, registration or licence, check the specific requirement rather than assuming a related course is sufficient.</p><h2>One master CV, several targeted applications</h2><p>Tailoring every application does not mean rewriting your employment history from the beginning each time. The more efficient approach is to maintain a master CV containing your full work history, qualifications, projects, training and achievements.</p><p>When a vacancy appears, create a copy and adjust the profile, skills, experience bullets and order of information. You might keep separate versions for customer service, administration and technical roles, updating them whenever you acquire new experience.</p><p>Save each application with a clear filename, such as <code>Amina-Nakato-CV-Administrative-Assistant.pdf</code>. Before submitting it, check that the employer's name and job title are correct, the dates agree with your other documents, and the file follows the requested format. If the employer asks for a Word document rather than a PDF, follow that instruction.</p><p>Keep personal information proportionate, too. Your name, telephone number, professional email address and relevant location are generally sufficient contact details. There is no need to routinely place your National Identification Number, passport number or bank details on a CV. Provide sensitive documentation only when legitimately required through an appropriate recruitment process.</p><p>A tailored CV should still be recognisably yours. Your employment dates, qualifications and responsibilities must remain consistent across applications, cover letters and application forms.</p><h2>The final test is relevance</h2><p>Before sending a CV, read the vacancy again and then read your document as if you were the recruiter. Can you quickly identify the experience, skills and qualifications that make you a credible candidate? Are the strongest examples easy to find? Can you explain and substantiate every claim?</p><p>If the answer is no, the document probably needs another revision.</p><p>Tailoring cannot compensate for every skills gap, an unsuitable qualification or a lack of experience where the employer insists on it. Nor can it guarantee an interview. Recruitment decisions depend on the applicant pool, the employer's priorities and factors beyond your control.</p><p>What it can do is prevent relevant experience from being overlooked because it was buried beneath generic language or unrelated information.</p><p>Your CV is a record of your professional history, but it is also an argument for your suitability. Each vacancy gives you a different reason to make that argument. The facts should never change; the emphasis should.</p><div aria-label="Article tags"><span>CV</span><span>CV writing</span><span>job search</span><span>career advice</span><span>Job Applications</span></div><div>Content originally published on SSEGU.
