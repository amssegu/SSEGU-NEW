<p>  </p><div><a href="https://blogger.googleusercontent.com/img/b/R29vZ2xl/AVvXsEiop39QQ6W6AqZRuHbcznP2F5XqAdpE34hWYI6Au6QSq1GkastEyuQYiI1nwmWQArDS1PtFrD7NYx9ulHFNt_wouI9WLzTVhzhlj04JUkrIFvFEetz11k62IwVnYdo9Gb7P3jOgl0h5KxDh5CIW9h3fY3opJ6xFpQ9DfA4qyT5M99ZY5OBfnTNFM_3mvKE/s963/cv.jpg"></a></div><br><div><br></div><div>A good CV will not guarantee you a job. It should, however, make it easy for an employer or recruiter to understand <strong>who you are, what you can do, and why you are relevant to the vacancy</strong>.</div><p>That matters in Uganda, where job seekers may apply through company websites, recruitment platforms such as BrighterMonday, email, referrals, recruitment agencies, or specific government application systems.</p><p>The goal is not to make your CV look impressive at first glance. The goal is to make the right information easy to find and easy to believe.</p><p>Here is how to write one that does exactly that.</p><h2>1. Start with the job advert, not your old CV</h2><p>One of the biggest mistakes job seekers make is creating one CV and sending the exact same document to every employer.</p><p>Instead, start with the vacancy.</p><p>Read the advert carefully and identify:</p><ul><li><p>The job title</p></li><li><p>Required academic qualifications</p></li><li><p>Professional qualifications or licences</p></li><li><p>Required years of experience</p></li><li><p>Technical skills and software</p></li><li><p>Main responsibilities</p></li><li><p>Industry knowledge</p></li><li><p>Location</p></li><li><p>Application instructions</p></li><li><p>Any documents the employer specifically requests</p></li></ul><p>Then compare those requirements with your own experience.</p><p>For example, suppose an employer is looking for a <strong>Sales Representative</strong> who needs customer-service skills, field sales experience and knowledge of Microsoft Excel.</p><p>Your CV should make those points easy to find if you genuinely have them.</p><p>If you have worked in a shop, handled customers, maintained sales records or used Excel, do not bury that information halfway down the page.</p><h3>Keep a master CV, then tailor it</h3><p>A master CV can contain your complete work history, projects, training and skills.</p><p>For each vacancy, create a shorter version that prioritises the experience most relevant to that particular job.</p><p>You do not need to rewrite everything from scratch. Often, changing the professional summary, rearranging skills and adjusting a few experience bullets is enough.</p><h2>2. Put your contact information at the top</h2><p>Your name and contact details should be immediately visible.</p><p>A practical format is:</p><p><strong>Enoch Muwanguzi</strong><br>Kampala, Uganda<br>+256 7XX XXX XXX<br><a href="mailto:enoch.muwanguzi@email.com">enoch.me@email.com</a><br>LinkedIn: linkedin.com/in/yourname</p><p>Include:</p><ul><li><p>Full name</p></li><li><p>Active phone number</p></li><li><p>Professional email address</p></li><li><p>Town or city</p></li><li><p>LinkedIn or portfolio link where relevant</p></li></ul><p>You generally do not need to put your complete residential address on a CV. Your city or district is usually enough unless the employer specifically asks for more information.</p><p>For jobs in Kampala, for example, writing <strong>Kampala, Uganda</strong> gives an employer useful location information without unnecessarily exposing your home address.</p><h3>Use a professional email address</h3><p>An email such as:</p><p><code inline="">johnokello@gmail.com</code></p><p>is perfectly adequate.</p><p>An address such as:</p><p><code inline="">bigbossjohn2004@gmail.com</code></p><p>may not make the best professional impression.</p><p>You do not need an expensive domain or elaborate email signature. A simple address based on your name is enough.</p><h2>3. Write a short professional summary</h2><p>Your professional summary should quickly tell the employer what kind of candidate they are looking at.</p><p>Keep it to roughly <strong>two to four lines</strong>.</p><p>Avoid vague statements such as:</p><blockquote><p>“I am a hardworking, self-motivated and God-fearing individual seeking a challenging opportunity where I can utilise my skills.”</p></blockquote><p>There is nothing particularly wrong with being hardworking or motivated, but thousands of CVs say exactly the same thing.</p><p>Instead, connect your experience to the role.</p><p>For example:</p><blockquote><p><strong>Customer service assistant with two years of experience supporting customers, processing transactions and maintaining daily records. Comfortable using Microsoft Excel and point-of-sale systems, with a strong focus on accurate and courteous service.</strong></p></blockquote><p>For a recent graduate:</p><blockquote><p><strong>Recent Bachelor of Information Technology graduate with practical experience in web development through academic and personal projects. Skilled in HTML, CSS, JavaScript and Git, with a portfolio of small web applications. Seeking an entry-level software or web development role.</strong></p></blockquote><p>The second example is more useful because it tells the employer <strong>what the applicant can actually do</strong>.</p><h2>4. Put relevant skills where employers can find them</h2><p>Do not simply create a giant list of every skill you have ever heard of.</p><p>Choose skills that are relevant to the vacancy.</p><p>For example, a CV for an accounts assistant might include:</p><p><strong>Technical skills</strong></p><ul><li><p>Microsoft Excel</p></li><li><p>QuickBooks</p></li><li><p>Bookkeeping</p></li><li><p>Bank reconciliation</p></li><li><p>Financial reporting</p></li></ul><p><strong>Professional skills</strong></p><ul><li><p>Record keeping</p></li><li><p>Attention to detail</p></li><li><p>Customer communication</p></li><li><p>Time management</p></li></ul><p>A junior developer might instead list:</p><ul><li><p>HTML</p></li><li><p>CSS</p></li><li><p>JavaScript</p></li><li><p>React</p></li><li><p>Git</p></li><li><p>SQL</p></li><li><p>Responsive web design</p></li></ul><p>The important rule is simple:</p><p><strong>Do not list a skill you cannot comfortably demonstrate in an interview or practical assessment.</strong></p><p>If you write “advanced Excel”, be prepared to explain what you can actually do with Excel.</p><h2>5. Turn work experience into evidence</h2><p>This is where many otherwise decent CVs become rather dull.</p><p>Compare:</p><p><strong>Weak</strong></p><blockquote><p>Responsible for customer service.</p></blockquote><p>with:</p><p><strong>Better</strong></p><blockquote><p>Assisted customers with product enquiries, orders and complaints while maintaining accurate daily sales records.</p></blockquote><p>The second version explains what you actually did.</p><p>Even better, when you have genuine figures:</p><blockquote><p>Assisted an average of 30+ customers per day and maintained daily sales records using Microsoft Excel.</p></blockquote><p>Numbers can make achievements more concrete, but <strong>do not invent statistics simply because they look impressive</strong>.</p><p>If you do not know the exact number, describe the work honestly.</p><h3>A useful formula</h3><p>For each important bullet, think:</p><p><strong>Action + task + result or purpose</strong></p><p>For example:</p><blockquote><p>Prepared weekly stock reports in Excel to help the supervisor identify low-stock items.</p></blockquote><blockquote><p>Created social media posts and maintained a weekly content calendar for a student organisation.</p></blockquote><blockquote><p>Supported five new staff members during their first week by explaining workplace procedures and equipment operation.</p></blockquote><p>The result does not always have to be a dramatic percentage increase. Showing responsibility, scope and practical output is valuable too.</p><h2>6. Include internships, volunteering and serious projects</h2><p>Being inexperienced does not mean having nothing to put on a CV.</p><p>If you are a recent graduate or looking for your first formal job, relevant experience can come from:</p><ul><li><p>Internships</p></li><li><p>Industrial training</p></li><li><p>Volunteering</p></li><li><p>University projects</p></li><li><p>Student organisations</p></li><li><p>Freelance work</p></li><li><p>Personal projects</p></li><li><p>Community work</p></li><li><p>Temporary or casual work</p></li><li><p>Family-business responsibilities, where genuinely relevant</p></li></ul><p>A computer science graduate with no formal developer job might include a project such as:</p><blockquote><p><strong>Student Expense Tracker | Personal Project</strong><br>Built a web application for recording expenses and categorising monthly spending using JavaScript and local storage. Designed the interface for mobile and desktop use.</p></blockquote><p>That gives the employer something concrete to discuss.</p><p>“Computer skills” tells very little.</p><p>A working project tells much more.</p><h2>7. Structure your education section clearly</h2><p>For most Ugandan job applications, education is an important part of the CV, particularly for graduate, internship and entry-level positions.</p><p>A simple format works well:</p><p><strong>Bachelor of Information Technology</strong><br>Makerere University<br>2023–2026</p><p>For qualifications that have specific professional relevance, include them clearly.</p><p>You may also include:</p><ul><li><p>Diploma</p></li><li><p>Certificate</p></li><li><p>Professional certification</p></li><li><p>Relevant short courses</p></li><li><p>Professional licences</p></li><li><p>Relevant academic projects</p></li></ul><p>If you are still studying, say so.</p><p>For example:</p><blockquote><p><strong>Bachelor of Computer Science — In progress</strong><br>University of the People<br>Expected completion: 2027</p></blockquote><p>Do not claim a qualification you have not completed.</p><h2>8. Keep the CV simple and readable</h2><p>A CV does not need to look like a nightclub flyer.</p><p>Use:</p><ul><li><p>Clear section headings</p></li><li><p>Consistent fonts</p></li><li><p>Reasonable font size</p></li><li><p>Short paragraphs</p></li><li><p>Bullet points</p></li><li><p>Consistent dates</p></li><li><p>Adequate white space</p></li><li><p>Simple formatting</p></li></ul><p>A clean CV is usually safer than one overloaded with graphics, icons, multiple colours, progress bars and decorative elements.</p><p>This is particularly important when applying online because you cannot control how an employer's device, software or recruitment platform will display your document.</p><p>BrighterMonday's own Uganda CV guidance recommends a clear, scannable CV focused on relevant skills, experience and achievements.</p><h3>Should you use a CV photo?</h3><p>Do not assume that every Ugandan employer requires a photograph.</p><p>A photo may be appropriate for some roles or where the employer specifically requests one, but it is not necessary for every application.</p><p>If the vacancy does not ask for a photo, prioritise useful professional information instead.</p><h2>9. How long should a CV be in Uganda?</h2><p>There is no universal legal or recruitment rule saying that every CV must be exactly one or two pages.</p><p>For a student, recent graduate or applicant with limited experience, <strong>one page is often enough</strong>.</p><p>For someone with several years of relevant experience, <strong>two pages may be more appropriate</strong>.</p><p>An experienced professional may legitimately need more.</p><p>The better question is:</p><p><strong>Does every section help the employer evaluate me for this job?</strong></p><p>If a three-page CV contains two pages of irrelevant information, making it shorter will probably improve it.</p><h2>10. Be careful with personal information</h2><p>A CV contains personal data, so avoid putting sensitive information on it simply because you have seen it on someone else's CV.</p><p>Think carefully before including:</p><ul><li><p>National ID number</p></li><li><p>Bank account details</p></li><li><p>Mobile Money PINs</p></li><li><p>Passwords</p></li><li><p>Full residential address</p></li><li><p>Unnecessary financial information</p></li><li><p>Medical information</p></li><li><p>Private family information</p></li></ul><p>Uganda's Data Protection and Privacy Act regulates the collection and processing of personal data, including information that can identify an individual.</p><p>That does not mean you should refuse to provide legitimate information when an employer requires it as part of a genuine recruitment process. It means you should <strong>share personal information deliberately and with a legitimate recipient</strong>, rather than attaching every document you own to every job application.</p><p>If an alleged recruiter asks for your bank details, National ID or other sensitive information before you have established that the vacancy and organisation are genuine, stop and verify first.</p><h2>11. Do not lie about qualifications or experience</h2><p>A CV should present your strongest truthful case, not create a fictional version of you.</p><p>Do not:</p><ul><li><p>Invent employment</p></li><li><p>Alter dates to hide gaps</p></li><li><p>Claim someone else's project as your own</p></li><li><p>Inflate your job title</p></li><li><p>Claim qualifications you have not earned</p></li><li><p>List software you have never used</p></li><li><p>Invent achievements</p></li><li><p>Provide fake referees</p></li></ul><p>If you have a gap in employment, you do not necessarily need a dramatic explanation on the CV.</p><p>If the gap is relevant, explain it briefly and honestly during the application or interview.</p><p>Integrity matters particularly for roles where qualifications, professional registration or licences are part of the requirements.</p><h2>12. Should you include referees?</h2><p>Follow the employer's instructions.</p><p>If the vacancy requests referees, provide the requested information.</p><p>If it does not, you can leave referees off the main CV and provide them when requested.</p><p>If you do include them, use people who genuinely know your work, education or professional ability.</p><p>Ask their permission first.</p><p>A referee should not discover that they have apparently recommended you when a recruiter calls them unexpectedly.</p><p>A simple entry could look like:</p><p><strong>Jane Namusoke</strong><br>Former Supervisor, ABC Ltd<br>+256 7XX XXX XXX<br><a href="mailto:jane.namusoke@email.com">jane.namusoke@email.com</a></p><p>Do not invent a referee merely to fill space.</p><h2>13. Name your CV properly</h2><p>Avoid filenames such as:</p><p><code inline="">finalCVnew2.pdf</code></p><p>or:</p><p><code inline="">CV latest FINAL.pdf</code></p><p>Use something that immediately identifies the document.</p><p>For example:</p><p><code inline="">Enoch_Muwanguzi_CV.pdf</code></p><p>For a tailored application:</p><p><code inline="">Enoch_Muwanguzi_CV_Sales_Assistant.pdf</code></p><p>This is a tiny detail, but it makes your application look organised and makes the document easier for a recruiter to identify.</p><h2>14. PDF is usually a good choice, but follow the advert</h2><p>If the employer asks for a PDF, send a PDF.</p><p>If the application specifically requests a Word document, send the requested format.</p><p>Do not blindly follow the rule “always send PDF”.</p><p>Before sending a PDF:</p><ol><li><p>Open it.</p></li><li><p>Check that the formatting has not shifted.</p></li><li><p>Check your phone number and email.</p></li><li><p>Test links.</p></li><li><p>Check page breaks.</p></li><li><p>Make sure your name appears correctly.</p></li><li><p>Confirm that the file is the final version.</p></li></ol><p>A surprisingly large number of application mistakes happen because people send the wrong file.</p><h2>15. Pay attention to how you apply</h2><p>A strong CV can still fail if you ignore the application instructions.</p><p>If the advert says:</p><blockquote><p>Send your CV and cover letter to <a href="mailto:recruitment@example.com">recruitment@example.com</a></p></blockquote><p>follow that instruction.</p><p>If it says:</p><blockquote><p>Apply through the company website</p></blockquote><p>use the company's application system.</p><p>If it asks for a particular subject line, use it.</p><p>If it asks for certified academic documents, do not assume your CV is enough.</p><p>This matters in Uganda's public sector as well. Government recruitment is increasingly supported by electronic human-resource systems, so applicants should follow the exact instructions attached to the particular vacancy rather than assuming every public-sector job uses the same application process. The Ministry of Public Service says its Human Capital Management system includes recruitment functions and integration for verification of public-service job applicants.</p><h2>16. Be careful when applying through job websites</h2><p>Job platforms can make the search much easier, but a listing appearing online does not automatically prove that the person behind it is genuine.</p><p>BrighterMonday Uganda, for example, says some vacancies are directly provided by employers while others are sourced from elsewhere. It specifically advises job seekers to verify employers and be cautious about requests for upfront payments or suspicious listings.</p><p>A few useful rules:</p><ul><li><p>Never pay someone simply to “secure” a job.</p></li><li><p>Research the organisation before sharing sensitive documents.</p></li><li><p>Check whether the employer has a legitimate website or other credible presence.</p></li><li><p>Apply through the official application channel where possible.</p></li><li><p>Be suspicious of unrealistic salaries or promises.</p></li><li><p>Never share passwords, PINs or banking credentials.</p></li><li><p>Verify recruitment agencies before handing over important documents.</p></li></ul><p>BrighterMonday also provides a reporting function for suspicious job listings.</p><h2>17. Your CV does not need to contain everything about you</h2><p>A CV is not your autobiography.</p><p>You do not need to list:</p><ul><li><p>Every school you attended</p></li><li><p>Every short course you have ever taken</p></li><li><p>Every hobby</p></li><li><p>Every responsibility from ten years ago</p></li><li><p>Generic personality adjectives</p></li><li><p>Unrelated personal information</p></li></ul><p>Include information that helps answer:</p><p><strong>“Why is this person worth considering for this particular vacancy?”</strong></p><p>For example, if you are applying for an accounting position, your ability to use Excel and prepare financial records is more useful than a paragraph about your love of football.</p><p>If you are applying for a graphic-design role, your portfolio may matter more than a long list of unrelated certificates.</p><p>Relevance beats volume.</p><h2>18. A simple CV structure you can follow</h2><p>For many job applications in Uganda, this structure is a good starting point:</p><h3>1. Name and contact details</h3><ul><li><p>Full name</p></li><li><p>Phone</p></li><li><p>Email</p></li><li><p>Location</p></li><li><p>LinkedIn or portfolio, if relevant</p></li></ul><h3>2. Professional summary</h3><p>Two to four lines explaining your professional background and target role.</p><h3>3. Key skills</h3><p>Five to ten relevant technical and professional skills.</p><h3>4. Work experience</h3><p>Start with your most recent relevant position.</p><p>For each role include:</p><ul><li><p>Job title</p></li><li><p>Employer</p></li><li><p>Location, if useful</p></li><li><p>Dates</p></li><li><p>Three to five strong bullet points</p></li></ul><h3>5. Education</h3><p>List relevant qualifications, starting with the most recent.</p><h3>6. Certifications or training</h3><p>Include relevant professional courses and certifications.</p><h3>7. Projects</h3><p>Useful for students, graduates, freelancers and people changing careers.</p><h3>8. Referees</h3><p>Only if requested or genuinely useful.</p><h2>19. Example: weak CV bullet vs strong CV bullet</h2><h3>Weak</h3><blockquote><p>Worked on company social media.</p></blockquote><h3>Better</h3><blockquote><p>Created and scheduled weekly social media content and maintained a content calendar for the organisation.</p></blockquote><h3>Stronger when you have genuine results</h3><blockquote><p>Created and scheduled weekly social media content, maintained a content calendar and tracked post engagement to guide future content.</p></blockquote><p>Notice that the stronger version does not need an invented “300% increase in engagement” to sound professional.</p><p><strong>Specificity is more valuable than exaggeration.</strong></p><h2>20. Final CV checklist before applying</h2><p>Before you click <strong>Send</strong>, check the following:</p><h3>Content</h3><ul><li><p>Does the CV match the job you are applying for?</p></li><li><p>Is the target role obvious?</p></li><li><p>Are the most relevant skills easy to find?</p></li><li><p>Have you shown what you actually did?</p></li><li><p>Have you included relevant projects or internships?</p></li></ul><h3>Accuracy</h3><ul><li><p>Are your dates correct?</p></li><li><p>Are your qualifications accurate?</p></li><li><p>Are company and institution names spelled correctly?</p></li><li><p>Are your phone number and email correct?</p></li><li><p>Are your referees genuine?</p></li></ul><h3>Formatting</h3><ul><li><p>Is the document easy to read on a phone?</p></li><li><p>Are headings consistent?</p></li><li><p>Are bullet points aligned?</p></li><li><p>Is there unnecessary decoration?</p></li><li><p>Does the PDF open correctly?</p></li></ul><h3>Application</h3><ul><li><p>Did you follow the employer's instructions?</p></li><li><p>Did you attach the correct documents?</p></li><li><p>Did you use the requested email subject?</p></li><li><p>Did you address the correct employer?</p></li><li><p>Did you save a copy of the application?</p></li></ul><h3>Security</h3><ul><li><p>Are you sending personal information only to a legitimate recipient?</p></li><li><p>Have you avoided unnecessary sensitive documents?</p></li><li><p>Has anyone asked you to pay money before receiving the job?</p></li></ul><p>If everything checks out, send it.</p><h2>21. Keep an application tracker</h2><p>If you are applying for many jobs, do not rely on your memory.</p><p>Create a simple spreadsheet with columns such as:</p><table><thead><tr><th>Date</th><th>Employer</th><th>Position</th><th>Application method</th><th>CV version</th><th>Status</th><th>Follow-up</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>15 Sept</td><td>ABC Ltd</td><td>Sales Assistant</td><td>Email</td><td>Sales CV</td><td>Applied</td><td>25 Sept</td></tr><tr><td>16 Sept</td><td>XYZ NGO</td><td>Programme Assistant</td><td>Website</td><td>NGO CV</td><td>Applied</td><td>Check portal</td></tr></tbody></table><p>This becomes particularly useful when you have several versions of your CV.</p><p>It also prevents the embarrassing situation of applying to the same company twice with completely different information.</p><h2>What makes a CV stand out to employers in Uganda?</h2><p>There is no magic phrase, font or template that guarantees an interview.</p><p>A strong CV generally does five things well:</p><ol><li><p><strong>It is relevant to the vacancy.</strong></p></li><li><p><strong>It clearly shows what the applicant can do.</strong></p></li><li><p><strong>It provides evidence rather than empty claims.</strong></p></li><li><p><strong>It is easy to scan and understand.</strong></p></li><li><p><strong>It is accurate and professionally presented.</strong></p></li></ol><p>Job platforms themselves increasingly give applicants tools to maintain online profiles alongside CVs. BrighterMonday, for example, allows job seekers to create profiles, upload CVs and apply for vacancies through its platform.</p><p>Your CV is therefore only one part of the job search. Your online profile, portfolio, application message, interview performance and professional reputation can all matter as well.</p><h2>Final takeaway</h2><p>Do not try to make your CV look impressive to everyone.</p><p>Make it <strong>relevant to the person reading it</strong>.</p><p>Start with the vacancy. Identify what the employer needs. Put your strongest matching evidence near the top. Explain what you actually did instead of filling the page with generic adjectives. Keep the design clean, tell the truth, protect your personal information and follow the employer's application instructions.</p><p>A good CV does not shout.</p><p>It makes the right evidence difficult to miss.</p><p><strong>Tailor your CV to one real vacancy today, read it once on your phone, and remove anything that does not help the employer understand why you should be considered.</strong></p><div aria-label="Article tags"><span>career advice</span><span>CV writing</span><span>Employment Uganda</span><span>Fresh Graduates</span><span>Job Applications</span><span>job search</span><span>Jobs in Uganda</span><span>CV</span></div><div>Content originally published on SSEGU.
