<p> When many of us think about Uganda’s healthcare system, the picture that emerges is one of overwhelmed hospitals, mothers crammed into under-equipped maternity wards, patients turned away due to medicine shortages, and exhausted nurses struggling to make ends meet. It’s a story many Ugandans know all too well, a system hanging on by a thread.</p><h5>A Fragile Health System Held Together by Dedication</h5><p data-end="1454" data-start="1204">Uganda’s healthcare system is a paradox. On paper, it’s a decentralized model designed to bring services closer to the people, from Health Centre I at the village level to national referral hospitals in cities. But in practice, the reality is grim:</p><ul data-end="1958" data-start="1455"><li data-end="1589" data-start="1455"><p data-end="1589" data-start="1457"><strong data-end="1496" data-start="1457">One doctor for every 7,272 Ugandans</strong>, a shocking statistic that highlights the severe shortage of skilled health professionals.</p></li><li data-end="1732" data-start="1590"><p data-end="1732" data-start="1592"><strong data-end="1618" data-start="1592">Underfunded facilities, </strong>often, patients have to buy their own medical supplies, and hospitals routinely run out of essential medicines.</p></li><li data-end="1840" data-start="1733"><p data-end="1840" data-start="1735"><strong data-end="1767" data-start="1735">Overburdened maternity wards, </strong>where mothers sometimes share beds, or worse, give birth on the floor.</p></li><li data-end="1958" data-start="1841"><p data-end="1958" data-start="1843"><strong data-end="1875" data-start="1843">Underpaid healthcare workers, </strong>nurses, and doctors who earn meager salaries and sometimes go months without pay.</p></li></ul><p data-end="2177" data-start="1960">This broken system hits hardest in rural areas, where <strong data-end="2050" data-start="2014">76% of Uganda’s population lives</strong>. Many villages have no doctor, no nurse, and no clinic within walking distance. That’s where Community Health Workers step in.</p><p>According to WHO data, the country has one of the lowest doctor-to-patient ratios in the world, with just one doctor for every 25,000 Ugandans. Public hospitals often run out of essential drugs. Nurses go months without pay. Pregnant women give birth on floors or in overcrowded wards with barely any medical assistance.</p><blockquote><p>The first major issue I saw with Uganda’s healthcare system was the lack of healthcare workers and medical education. Uganda has one of the lowest doctor-to-patient ratios in the world with only one doctor available for every 25,000 people.<sup>6</sup> This ratio is below the World Health Organization (WHO) recommendation of 1 doctor per 1,000 people. An emergency physician I interviewed mentioned that he is one of only 15 emergency physicians in all of Uganda, a country with a population of 45 million - <a href="https://sites.brown.edu/publichealthjournal/2024/03/29/beyond-borders-a-glimpse-into-ugandas-healthcare-challenges-and-solutions/">A Glimpse into Uganda’s Healthcare Challenges and Solutions, Brown Undergraduate Journal of Public Health</a></p></blockquote><h6><span>Who Are Community Health Workers?</span></h6><p>In 2001, the Ugandan government introduced the Village Health Teams (VHTs) strategy, a community-based volunteer program aimed at bringing basic healthcare to the grassroots. VHTs are local residents selected by their communities and trained to offer basic health education, treat common illnesses, and refer serious cases to health centres.</p><p>They are not doctors. They are not nurses. But in villages where medical professionals are rare, they are <em>everything</em>.</p><p>Their responsibilities include:</p><ul><li>Educating households on hygiene, sanitation, and nutrition</li><li>Monitoring pregnancies and advising expectant mothers</li><li>Treating simple illnesses like malaria, diarrhea, and pneumonia in children</li><li>Encouraging immunization and family planning</li><li>Recording community health data and submitting reports to higher health authorities</li></ul><p>VHTs operate under the broader umbrella of Community Health Workers, a term that includes both volunteers and a newer cadre known as Community Health Extension Workers (CHEWs), who are salaried, professionally trained, and deployed at the parish level.</p><h5>The CHEW Initiative: Taking It a Step Further</h5><p>Recognizing the limitations of the VHT program, especially the lack of pay, burnout, and limited training, the government launched the Community Health Extension Workers (CHEWs) program in 2022. CHEWs are more specialized and better resourced. While VHTs remain volunteers, CHEWs are salaried and receive more extensive training to handle a wider range of health issues.</p><p>They provide services such as:</p><ul><li>Maternal and child health education</li><li>Immunization campaigns</li><li>Basic diagnosis and treatment</li><li>Disease surveillance and response</li><li>Health promotion and data collection</li></ul><p>By integrating CHEWs into the <a href="https://info.ssegu.com/lifestyle/health/">healthcare system</a>, Uganda aims to professionalize community healthcare and improve service quality in areas that are chronically underserved.</p><p><a href="https://info.ssegu.com/living-with-hiv-aids-in-the-present-days-a-message-of-hope-to-a-new-patient/">Living with HIV/AIDS in the Present Day: A Message of Hope to a New Patient</a></p><h5>The Power of Digital Tools</h5><p>In recent years, organizations like <a href="https://livinggoods.org/">Living Goods</a> have introduced mobile apps like <a href="https://www.google.com/search?q=apps+like+Smart+Health&amp;sourceid=chrome&amp;ie=UTF-8">Smart Health</a> that have revolutionized how CHWs operate. These digital tools allow CHWs to:</p><ul><li>Log patient visits and track medication adherence</li><li>Access diagnostic checklists and treatment protocols</li><li>Send data directly to health authorities in real-time</li><li>Receive alerts and reminders for follow-ups</li></ul><p>With smartphones in hand, CHWs can now provide care that is not only timely but also data-driven. This has dramatically improved health outcomes in areas where traditional health systems simply don’t reach.</p><h5>Real Impact on the Ground</h5><p>Here’s the thing: Uganda’s healthcare system may be limping, but Community Health Workers are the crutches helping it stand. Their impact is real, and here’s how they’re making a difference: Consider the case of Namayingo District, where malaria and diarrhea were once leading causes of child mortality. After training and deploying VHTs equipped with diagnostic tools and antimalarial kits, the number of under-five deaths dropped significantly.</p><p>In another village in Mpigi, pregnant women now get reminders about antenatal visits through CHWs who track their progress using mobile apps. The result? Fewer maternal deaths and healthier newborns.</p><p>These stories are not isolated. Across Uganda, CHWs are the first, and often the only, point of contact between the healthcare system and the community.</p><p data-end="4161" data-start="3910">Health isn’t just about treating diseases; it’s about preventing them. CHWs teach communities about clean water, sanitation, mosquito net use, and proper nutrition, helping reduce the spread of preventable diseases like malaria, diarrhea, and cholera.</p><h5>Challenges That Still Need Addressing</h5><p>But it’s not all smooth sailing, despite their importance, CHWs face significant hurdles:</p><ul><li data-end="5005" data-start="4881"><p data-end="5005" data-start="4883"><strong data-end="4904" data-start="4883">Lack of resources</strong>: Many work without basic tools like gloves, thermometers, or even bicycles to reach distant homes.</p></li><li data-end="5114" data-start="5006"><p data-end="5114" data-start="5008"><strong data-end="5031" data-start="5008">Inadequate training</strong>: VHTs often get a few days of training and are expected to handle complex cases.</p></li><li data-end="5245" data-start="5115"><p data-end="5245" data-start="5117"><strong data-end="5136" data-start="5117">No pay for VHTs</strong>: While CHEWs are salaried, most VHTs work as unpaid volunteers, leading to burnout and high dropout rates.</p></li><li data-end="5363" data-start="5246"><p data-end="5363" data-start="5248"><strong data-end="5271" data-start="5248">Limited supervision</strong>: Without proper oversight, CHWs may struggle to maintain quality and consistency in care.</p></li></ul><p>These challenges not only affect morale but also reduce the impact CHWs can have. If Uganda is to meet its universal health coverage goals, CHWs must be better supported.</p><div>Content originally published on SSEGU.
